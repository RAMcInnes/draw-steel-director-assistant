# Draw Steel — What a Negotiation Is

*Source: Draw Steel Heroes, Chapter 11: Negotiation, book pp. 283–284.*

## Purpose

Negotiation gives the heroes a chance to get what they want without combat — or at least without further combat. Examples include negotiating with a king to obtain military support against an incursion of demons, talking a bandit leader into redirecting her raids, or convincing an archmage to grant access to a secret library. Convincing a lich to lend the party the *Codex Mortis*, halting a dragon's attack on a wizard's tower, or talking enemy commanders into standing down all qualify.

Think of negotiation as another subsystem like combat, exploration, or investigation. It is a framework for roleplaying — meant to be read by both players and Director — and may need a session or two to become familiar.

Negotiation tracks the NPC's interest in the heroes' arguments and their patience, so the Director knows what the NPC is willing to offer and when the scene ends. The framework supports rather than replaces roleplay (though a group that doesn't enjoy improvised dialogue can let the rules carry the scene).

## When to Negotiate

A negotiation only happens when an NPC has both:

- An **interest** in negotiating with the heroes, and
- A **reason to not** simply accept whatever the heroes propose.

That internal tension — wanting to help but being constrained — is what makes a negotiation. Example: the heroes ask a king to send his army into a neighboring kingdom against a demon incursion. He wants the incursion stopped, but he doesn't want to risk his soldiers defending foreigners while leaving his own people undefended. That conflict is the negotiation.

Heroes are **not** expected to use negotiation rules every time they want to convince an NPC. A captured cultist coughing up information needs a single Presence test (Lie skill) or Might test (Intimidate). Flirting with the local alchemist for a free Healing Potion is a single Presence test (Flirt). Negotiations are reserved for high-stakes scenes — typically the whole party engaging one or more important named NPCs who can provide information, items, services, or support that dramatically changes the adventure: an item of great power, a retainer or companion, the services of an influential organization or nation, or a plot-twisting piece of information.

To negotiate successfully, heroes must make persuasive arguments. *"Do it or we kill you"* is a threat — a single Might test using Intimidate — and is **not** a negotiating tactic.

## Limits of Negotiation

Some players treat the negotiation rules as mind control. They are not. Limits include:

- **Character is not changed.** No argument convinces the vile Lord Syuul to give up evil and become a gardener. A negotiation can't make a queen hand over her crown and name the heroes the new rulers, or get a dragon to fork over their entire hoard. Negotiations only work when the heroes ask for something the NPC is **willing to seriously consider** giving them.

- **Reframing, not rewriting.** Heroes are trying to make an NPC understand how behaving differently would actually be **in character** for them. Convincing a loyal lieutenant of an evil boss to reconsider their ways — a classic dramatic trope — works because the heroes are arguing *"this is not who you are."* They are not changing the NPC's identity; they are showing the NPC their current path is out of character.

- **"Just do what we tell you, or else"** is not negotiation. Players who open with that posture are likely to lose the negotiation before it begins.

## The Threat of Violence

In the real world, ambassadors don't typically come to fistfights. But this is heroic fantasy: the PCs are armed to the teeth and able to alter reality with their minds. The threat of violence is **already implied** — everyone in the room knows the characters could draw steel at any moment.

The Director assumes that underlying potential is factored into every negotiation. If the heroes choose to bring the threat of violence to the **forefront** — explicit threats, weapons drawn — they have left negotiation and entered a different kind of scene. It's probably time to draw steel.

Negotiation is about persuading someone to help **willingly** because the heroes have convinced them that meeting their objectives is wise, logical, or makes them look good. A hero can absolutely threaten violence and force compliance, but a threatened NPC is not willingly cooperating. They are complying only while the threat is present, and will revert to prior behavior the moment they think they can.
